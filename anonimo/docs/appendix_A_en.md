# Supplementary material — Appendix A (English)


## Appendix A. Methodological details

This appendix supplements the methodology section of the article with the details of each step. Tables S1 to S3 follow the appendix.

### A1. Search query and limits of the title search

The search was run on document titles, because the presence of the term in the title indicates that a document takes the ecosystem concept as its object of study rather than mentioning it in passing. The query was `TITLE(ecosystem* AND NOT ("ecosystem service*" OR "ecosystem health" OR "ecosystem restoration" OR "marine ecosystem*" OR "forest ecosystem*" OR "aquatic ecosystem*" OR "coastal ecosystem*" OR "soil ecosystem*" OR "ecosystem function*")) AND (LIMIT-TO(SUBJAREA, "BUSI") OR LIMIT-TO(SUBJAREA, "DECI") OR LIMIT-TO(SUBJAREA, "SOCI"))`. In plain terms, the query retrieves titles containing *ecosystem* or its derivatives, except those using the expressions most common in ecology (ecosystem services, health, restoration and functions, and marine, forest, aquatic, coastal and soil ecosystems), in documents classified under business, management and accounting, decision sciences or social sciences. The query was run on 22 September 2026 and returned 19,419 records published between 1961 and 2027.

A title search is not enough to delimit the organizational literature, for two reasons. First, the exclusions and the subject-area restriction do not filter out ecological research, because Scopus assigns many journals to several subject areas; the records include, for instance, studies of lakes, glaciers or fisheries. Second, the term is used in organization studies with very different meanings, and a document can adopt it without engaging with any of the literatures that developed it. Membership of the corpus was therefore decided by a citation-based criterion, described next.

### A2. Starting streams, seed authors and the threshold of the rule

We started from seven streams: business, innovation, entrepreneurial, platform, service, software and knowledge ecosystems. These are the types distinguished by the reviews and typologies of the field. The comparative reviews synthesized by Cobben et al. (2022) agree on business and innovation ecosystems and add, depending on the case, knowledge, entrepreneurial, platform and service ecosystems. Scaringella and Radziwon (2018) concentrate on innovation, entrepreneurial, knowledge and business ecosystems, and identify service ecosystems and technological or digital ecosystems as cross-cutting concepts. Thomas and Autio (2020) also include platform and technology ecosystems. The software stream covers this last variant, which developed within software engineering (Manikas & Hansen, 2013). Industrial ecosystems were left out because they belong to industrial ecology rather than to organization studies (Scaringella & Radziwon, 2018). These seven streams serve to delimit the corpus and are not a hypothesis about its structure: the streams analyzed later are identified from the citation network and need not coincide with them.

For each stream, we chose as seeds between four and eight authors of works that those reviews treat as foundational or as key references. Examples are Moore (1993) and Iansiti and Levien (2004a) for business ecosystems; Adner (2006), Adner and Kapoor (2010) and Granstrand and Holgersson (2020) for innovation ecosystems; Isenberg (2010), Stam (2015), Spigel (2017) and Acs et al. (2017) for entrepreneurial ecosystems; Gawer and Cusumano (2002) and Jacobides et al. (2018) for platform ecosystems; and Vargo and Lusch (2004, 2016) for service ecosystems. The software stream includes the authors who proposed and reviewed that notion, such as Bosch, Jansen and Manikas (Manikas & Hansen, 2013). The knowledge stream includes the authors of Clarysse et al. (2014) and of Carayannis and Campbell (2009), which the reviews cite as references for that type, together with Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff (2000), whose triple helix is the antecedent from which that literature is barely distinguishable (Thomas & Autio, 2020). Table 1 summarizes the streams, the typologies that recognize them and their seed authors; Table S1 presents the most cited work of each author and the effect of removing that author from the list.

An independent check supports the selection. For each stream we took the search records that name that type of ecosystem in the title (for example, *entrepreneurial ecosystem*) and counted how often they cite each seed author. In six of the seven streams, between two thirds and nine in ten of those records cite at least one seed author of their stream (Table 1). In addition, 28 of the 41 seed authors are among the ten authors most often cited as first author by the records of their type, and several of the others, such as Levien, Kapoor and Van Alstyne, appear mainly as co-authors of the cited works (Table S1). The exception is the knowledge stream: only 32% of the documents presented as studies of knowledge ecosystems cite any of its seeds, because that smaller literature relies on the canon of business and innovation ecosystems and on works such as Järvi et al. (2018), which are not among its seeds. The weight of this stream in the corpus is small: removing all its seeds takes 178 records (3.6%) out of the core.

The requirement of two seeds from the same stream has two justifications. A single citation may be incidental (a mention of Moore in the introduction, for example), whereas two authors from the same stream indicate that the document engages with that literature. And requiring them within one stream, rather than in any combination, keeps the rule from presupposing what the study seeks to measure: if documents were admitted for citing both an entrepreneurship author and a strategy author, the corpus would be biased by construction toward connections between streams. The comparison with alternative thresholds supports this choice (Table S2). With a single seed, 1,529 more records would enter, and titles with ecological vocabulary would rise from 20 to 143, including ecological studies proper; with three seeds from the same stream, 1,238 records would leave, almost a quarter of the total.

This rule admitted 5,006 records, which form the core of the corpus. Another 3,103 cited only one seed, or had an organizational title but cited no seed, 1,090 had no reference list in Scopus and 10,220 were discarded (Figure 1). Three results indicate that the rule serves its purpose. Only 20 core documents have ecological vocabulary in the title, and they are organizational studies of sectors such as fishing or forestry, or use that vocabulary figuratively. Of the 2,258 records with a reference list that name a type of organizational ecosystem in the title and remain outside the core, 1,619 cite no seed author; that is, they use the term without engaging with any of the literatures that developed it. And no seed is decisive on its own: removing any one of them drops at most 3.4% of the core (Gawer, 169 records). Finally, the rule does not admit documents for citing Moore alone, so the examination of his canonization as an ancestor does not depend on the entry criterion.

### A3. Rescue layer, duplicates and composition of the corpus

The rule has a blind spot: because it examines the references of each document, it cannot admit the foundational texts, which predate the literature that cites them, or records whose references Scopus does not supply, such as *Harvard Business Review* articles. Adner (2006), one of the most cited texts in the field, fell outside the rule for that reason. To correct this, a second criterion was applied in the opposite direction: search records cited at least five times by core documents were added. Matches were sought by the first author's surname and year and verified against title words, so as not to confuse authors with common surnames. This rescue by incoming citation added 268 records, including Adner (2006), Moore (2006) and Isenberg (2016). The five-citation threshold is a design decision: a threshold of ten would have rescued 127 records and one of three, 441. The criterion was applied only once, so that the corpus would not snowball around what the core already cites, and the rescued records form an identifiable layer, which is excluded in one of the sensitivity tests.

After the two layers were combined, 12 duplicate records were removed. These came from 23 records sharing title and year, which formed 11 sets, mostly chapters reprinted in several edited volumes; the first record in each set was kept, and the citations received by the others were reassigned to it. The final corpus comprises 5,262 documents (4,998 from the core and 264 from the rescue layer), published between 1995 and 2026, plus two records dated 2027. It includes 3,701 journal articles (70%), 752 book chapters, 554 conference papers, 126 reviews and 129 documents of other types, and 97% are written in English. Output grew at a compound annual rate of 24.9% between 2006 and 2025, and 77% of the documents (4,050) were published from 2020 onwards. The three errata and the two retracted documents were excluded from the descriptive analyses; editorials were retained, because in this literature several of them are substantive pieces.

The title search sets the main boundary of the corpus. The texts that introduced the idea without using the term in their titles (Moore, 1993; Iansiti & Levien, 2004a; Isenberg, 2010) are not part of the corpus as documents, although they do appear as cited references, and only three records predate 2000. This affects the analyses differently. Co-citation, which works with references, reaches those antecedents. The analyses of the direct citation network, by contrast, begin with the oldest documents in the corpus, which are not necessarily the origins of each tradition.

### A4. Network construction and community detection

Building the direct citation network required a prior cleaning step. In the Scopus export, the semicolon separates both the references from one another and the authors within each reference, so splitting the field on semicolons turns every multi-author reference into several spurious ones: the 930,412 fragments this produces actually correspond to 399,963 references, 76.5 per document. The references were reconstructed from the year in parentheses with which each one ends. Each reference was then compared with the corpus documents: a document was considered to cite another when the first author's surname and the year matched and at least three title words also matched (or two, if they made up at least 60% of the cited document's title). The procedure processed 97% of the references and produced 51,452 links. As a check, the number of citations each document receives within the corpus matches the number computed by bibliometrix (correlation of 0.995 among the 50 most cited documents).

The research streams were identified as communities of this network. A community is a group of documents with many links among themselves and few with the rest. Direct citation is, moreover, the type of relation that best delimits research communities, ahead of co-citation and bibliographic coupling (Klavans & Boyack, 2017; Waltman & van Eck, 2012). Communities were obtained with the Leiden algorithm (Traag et al., 2019), which partitions the network so that the density of links within each group exceeds the density expected by chance by as much as possible. That difference is summarized by modularity, Q, which in this network is 0.474. The result is five large communities, eight groups of five documents or fewer and 191 documents with no links to the rest of the corpus; only the five large communities are treated as streams. Because the algorithm traverses the network in random order, it was repeated with four other initializations. Normalized mutual information (NMI), which equals 1 when two partitions are identical and 0 when they are unrelated, ranges from 0.70 to 0.87. The partition was computed before duplicates were removed and is retained for the remaining documents.

Each community was named after its most cited documents and the terms that predominate in its titles: entrepreneurship (1,691 documents; 79% carry *entrepreneurial ecosystem* in the title), strategy and platforms (1,659), innovation (1,045; 63% *innovation ecosystem*), service (498; 51% *service ecosystem*) and digital and data (156). The names are descriptive; the analyses use the partition, not the labels. These five communities do not coincide with the seven streams used to delimit the corpus, as the results show.

### A5. Co-citation

The first analysis asks which works each stream takes as its base. To answer it we used co-citation: two works are co-cited when they appear together in the bibliography of the same document, and the more often this happens, the closer the citing community considers them (Small, 1973). Groups of frequently co-cited works form the intellectual base of a literature. Unlike the direct citation network, co-citation works with cited works rather than with corpus documents, so it reaches antecedents that the search did not retrieve, such as Moore (1993), Van de Ven (1993) or Vargo and Lusch (2004).

To count co-citations correctly, each reference was reduced to a key formed by the first author's surname, the year and the first four words of the title, so that the different ways of citing the same work count as one. Moore (1993), for example, appeared with 237 different spellings, which the key brings together in 1,182 citations. The 300 most cited works were taken, and pairs appearing together in at least three documents were considered.

Among such highly cited works almost all appear together at some point, so what is informative is not whether two works are co-cited but how strongly. Each pair was therefore weighted by its association strength, which compares the observed co-citations with those expected given the number of citations of each work (van Eck & Waltman, 2009). The Leiden algorithm was applied to this weighted network, with a quality function equivalent to weighted modularity (Reichardt & Bornholdt, 2006). Thirty runs with different initializations were performed and the one with the highest quality was kept; the minimum NMI between runs is 0.91. The same three groups appear with 200 works (minimum NMI of 0.97) and with 500 (0.76). The clustering outputs of bibliometrix were not used in this step, because they group works by the existence of links and, in a network in which almost all works are connected to one another, return a single block.

Finally, for each community of the direct citation network we calculated the share of its citations to those 300 works that goes to each base, overall and by period. With the same keys we calculated, by period, the share of documents in the entrepreneurship community that cite Moore (1993), in order to date the moment at which that stream incorporated him as an ancestor.

### A6. Assortativity and integrative articles

The second analysis asks whether the streams cite one another and how that relationship changes over time. The measure is nominal assortativity (Newman, 2003), which summarizes in a single number the tendency of each stream to cite itself. It equals 0 when citations are distributed across streams without regard to membership, as they would be at random, and 1 when each stream cites only itself. Formally, r = (Σᵢ eᵢᵢ − Σᵢ aᵢbᵢ) / (1 − Σᵢ aᵢbᵢ), where eᵢⱼ is the share of citations going from stream *i* to stream *j*, and aᵢ and bᵢ are the shares of citations that stream *i* sends and receives. The term 1 − Σᵢ aᵢbᵢ is the share of cross-stream citations that would be expected if citations were distributed at random, and it serves as the benchmark for the observed share.

Assortativity was calculated for each year from 2014 (the first year with at least 100 internal citations), dating each citation by the year of the citing document, and for longer periods. Only citations to documents from earlier years were used, the same rule that path analysis requires. Two checks verify that the trend is not an artifact of the way the streams are defined. The first repeats the calculation with streams defined by the term in the title (*business*, *innovation*, *entrepreneurial*, *platform* or *service ecosystem*, among others), which does not depend on citation. The second excludes citations to documents by seed authors: because those authors take part in the admission rule, their texts could force the connection between streams.

On the same network we examined the role of the articles that propose to integrate the streams and are part of the corpus: Adner (2017), Jacobides et al. (2018), Kapoor (2018), Granstrand and Holgersson (2020) and Shipilov and Gawer (2020); Thomas and Autio (2020) is not in the corpus. For each one we calculated the share of the citations it receives that come from streams other than its own, and compared it with that of the 201 documents that receive 50 or more internal citations. Receiving citations from several streams is not enough, however, for an article to work as a bridge: for that, the streams would have to cite one another more after its publication. This share is therefore read together with the annual assortativity series.

### A7. Key-route main paths

The third analysis reconstructs the paths along which knowledge circulated. We used main path analysis, which Hummon and Doreian (1989) proposed for writing the history of a scientific theory from its citation network. The idea is as follows: if each citation transmits knowledge from the cited text to the citing one, a chain of citations (from one text to a later one, from that one to a more recent one, and so on) is a path of transmission, and the links traversed by the most paths are the main stretches of that history.

The analysis requires paths always to move forward in time. It therefore uses only the 50,273 links in which the cited document is from a year earlier than the citing one, and discards citations between documents of the same year or to documents dated later; the retained links connect 5,062 documents. In this network, a source is a document that cites no other document in the corpus, that is, a starting point, and a sink is a document that no one cites yet, that is, an end point. The network contains 102,233,606 paths from a source to a sink. Each link is assigned its search path count (SPC), which is the number of those paths that pass through it (Batagelj, 2003; Hummon & Doreian, 1989). A link with a high SPC is one that much of the knowledge circulating in the network must pass through.

The path with the highest SPC, called the main path, depends on the criterion used to build it: local search advances at each step along the link with the highest SPC, whereas global search chooses the path with the highest cumulative SPC (Liu & Lu, 2012). To avoid depending on a single path we used the key-route main paths of Liu and Lu (2012), which take the *k* links with the highest SPC and extend each one backward to a source and forward to a sink. The result is a small network that brings together the most traveled stretches. Networks were calculated with *k* = 1, 5, 10, 20 and 50, under both search criteria; Figure 4 presents the network for *k* = 20 with global search, and the trunks discussed appear under both criteria.

To measure the weight of each trunk, the origin of the paths was decomposed: for each community we counted how many of the paths that end in one of its documents start from each source, and the sources were grouped by community. The measure indicates structural weight in the network, not the number of authors who followed each tradition, and it favors early sources, because the number of paths grows multiplicatively with the length of the chains.

Finally, we read the reference lists of the documents that open each trunk and of the document in which the trunks converge, to establish which canon and which antecedents each one cites (Table 4). This qualitative step makes it possible to distinguish the adoption of the term from the adoption of an intellectual base, two processes that the network alone does not separate.

### A8. Brokerage roles and regression models

The capacity of a document to connect streams was measured with the brokerage roles of Gould and Fernandez (1989). The unit is each triad of documents in which a document B cites a document A and is cited by a third document C. If citations transmit knowledge, knowledge in that triad passes from A to C through B, which occupies the position of broker; triads in which C also cites A are excluded, because in them B is not necessary. The role of B depends on the streams to which the three documents belong. When A and C belong to different streams, B connects two streams, and it can do so in three ways: as a gatekeeper, when B belongs to the stream of C and brings knowledge from another stream into it; as a representative, when it belongs to the stream of A and carries that stream's knowledge to another; or as a liaison, when the three belong to different streams. The cross-stream brokerage of a document is the number of triads in which it occupies any of these three roles; the other two roles that Gould and Fernandez distinguish, coordinator and itinerant broker, connect documents of the same stream and are not counted. By construction, a document can broker only if others cite it, so the measure expresses in formal terms the idea that connecting streams requires being read by the streams being connected. This calculation uses the 51,452 links of the network. Documents outside the five large communities are treated as a single group, so that triads linking two different small groups are not counted as brokerage.

Documents with Latin American authors were compared with the rest through regression models estimated on the 4,463 documents published between 2013 and 2025, of which 339 have Latin American authors: 2013 is the year of the first such document and 2026 is incomplete. Each model compares documents that are similar in three respects that influence citations (year of publication, community and document type), which enter as fixed effects, and controls for the logarithm of one plus the number of internal references (except in the model for internal references themselves), because a document that cites more has more opportunities to occupy broker positions. Count variables (citations received, citations received from another stream, brokerage volume, streams cited and internal references) were modeled with Poisson regression, which with robust standard errors yields consistent estimates even when the data are overdispersed (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013). Binary variables (receiving any citation, brokering between streams at least once) were modeled with logistic regression. Citations received from another stream were modeled only as a count, as in Table 5. The share of references that a document directs to other streams was modeled with a logistic model for proportions, weighted by the number of references and without the control for internal references, because the share is already normalized. Standard errors are robust to heteroskedasticity (HC1; MacKinnon & White, 1985) and take the document as the unit. Results are reported as rate ratios for counts and as odds ratios for binary variables and proportions, with 95% confidence intervals; a value below 1 indicates a disadvantage relative to comparable documents. As a benchmark for the citations that documents with Latin American authors receive from the region itself, we used their weight among citing documents (those citing at least one other corpus document) from 2014 onwards, which is the share that would be expected if the origin of citations did not depend on the region.

### A9. Validation, sensitivity and reproducibility

Three checks support the calculations. The first is the agreement, already mentioned, between the internal citations of the network and those computed by bibliometrix. The second is independent replication: the main path computed in R coincides with the one computed in Python, and a script in base R reproduces the key-route main paths exactly. The third corrects a default output: the annual growth rate reported by bibliometrix (2.19%) was discarded because it depends on the endpoints of the series (one document in 1995 and two in 2027); the rate was instead calculated for 2006–2025.

In addition to the checks described for each analysis, two tests repeat the main analyses on subsets of the corpus, with the original community partition: the first excludes the rescue layer (4,998 documents) and the second keeps only journal articles and reviews (3,827 documents). The results hold (Table S3). Assortativity in 2020–2026 is 0.71 and 0.70, respectively, against 0.70 in the full corpus, and its increase since 2014 recurs in both subsets. The three co-citation bases reappear with almost identical composition (NMI of 1.00 and 0.93 relative to the original partition), and the innovation community still directs more than 80% of its citations to the strategy base. Both of the entrepreneurship stream's own trunks appear in the two key-route networks, and the link Malecki (2011) → Stam (2015) becomes the one with the second-highest SPC. Two changes are explained by the composition of each subset. Without the rescue layer Adner (2006) disappears, and the innovation trunk starts from Adner and Kapoor (2010), assigned to the strategy and platforms community; for this reason, the share of paths to entrepreneurship that originates in that community rises to 47%, while the share that originates in the stream itself goes from 40% to 43% (49% with articles only). With articles only, Theodoraki and Messeghem (2017), which Scopus classifies as a conference paper, leaves the network, and the entry of the Adner trunk into entrepreneurship shifts to other documents from 2016 and 2017 (Hayter, 2016; Brown & Mason, 2017; Spigel, 2017). The estimates for Latin America hold in both subsets; the only one that weakens is the probability of brokering for the set of documents with Latin American authors as a whole when only articles are considered (odds ratio of 0.73; 95% CI: 0.53–1.00). When communities are detected again in each subset, the agreement with the original partition (NMI of 0.70 and 0.69) is similar to the agreement produced by a change of initialization in the full corpus.

The calculations were carried out in Python 3.11, with the libraries pandas, python-igraph (Csárdi & Nepusz, 2006), leidenalg, scikit-learn and statsmodels (Seabold & Perktold, 2010), and in R 4.4.1, with bibliometrix 4.3.0 (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017). The cleaning and analysis scripts, the list of seeds, the record identifiers (EID and DOI) and the derived tables will be available in a public repository with a persistent identifier \[VERIFICAR: link and DOI\]. The complete Scopus records are not redistributed, because of the licensing terms of the database, but the identifiers allow them to be downloaded again.
